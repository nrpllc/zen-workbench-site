01The problem with diagrams

Most architecture tools draw boxes and arrows. A box-and-arrow diagram is a claim with no evidence: it says "this fits together" but it cannot tell you whether the database keeps up at peak, where the system breaks first, or what it costs in a year.

The moment a design leaves the whiteboard, its most important properties are quantitative — throughput, tail latency, saturation, storage growth, monthly spend, staffing, cycle time. A diagram is silent on all of them. So teams argue from intuition, and the design that wins is the one drawn most confidently, not the one that actually holds.

We built the Workbench on a different premise: the diagram and the model are the same artifact. When you place a node and wire it to another, you are not decorating a canvas — you are declaring a system whose behaviour we compute. Every node carries a cost, latency and capacity model. Every edge carries load. Every claim in the design carries a number, and every number is one you can open up, argue with, and tune.

A diagram asks you to trust it. A model shows its work.

02What we model

The Workbench treats an organisation and the system it runs as one connected graph — because Conway's Law says they mirror each other, and modelling one without the other hides half the risk.

On the architecture plane, each service — a database, a cache, a queue, a gateway, a stream processor, a rack, a cloud footprint — computes six outputs from its configuration and the offered load:

Latency
p50 · p99

Median and tail response time — because at scale the tail, not the mean, is the experience.

Throughput
served qps

What the node sustains, against a config-derived capacity ceiling.

Storage
GB retained

Data at rest, from record count, payload, indexing and replication.

Network
bytes/s

Ingest and cross-service traffic on the wire.

Cost
$ / month

Instances, storage, egress and licence, anchored to list prices.

Capacity
headroom

Where the design breaks first, and when growth gets it there.

On the organisation plane, the same rigor applies to value streams and people: cycle time and flow efficiency, Six Sigma yield, staffing and skill mix, meeting and artifact cost, and OKRs that bind to a metric the model already computes. Load emitted by field devices flows downstream along the edges and accumulates on every service, so an IoT fleet's rush-hour peak — not its daily average — is what sizes the ingest tier.

03How the models work

Every number is a back-of-envelope model you can read, not a black box. The guiding principle is the binding constraint: find the one resource that actually limits a node — CPU, memory, disk I/O, or a provisioned ceiling — and size against it, naming the trade-off of every dial.

Capacity as the smaller of two ceilings

A node's real throughput is bounded by whichever runs out first: its compute/parallelism ceiling or its disk-I/O ceiling, degraded by how much of the working set overflows RAM. A cache that doesn't fit its hot set isn't a cache — it's a slower database with extra steps, and the model says so by collapsing its effective hit ratio.

Queueing
p99 ≈ p50 · f(utilisation)

The tail widens non-linearly as a node approaches saturation — the M/M/1 intuition behind every latency curve.

Provisioned stores
qps = RU × reads/RU

DynamoDB & Cosmos rent throughput directly; capacity is the RU/s you buy, converted per the vendor definition.

Diurnal load
size for peak, not mean

Offered load follows a 24-hour curve; the busiest minute propagates downstream and sizes the fleet.

Above the per-node models sit the system laws that make a diagram of many services behave like a real one: Little's Law under every utilisation figure, Amdahl's and the Universal Scalability Law capping the gains from adding nodes, and the tail-at-scale effect where fanning out to many services means the slowest one governs.

04Every knob is real

A dial that renders in the tuning panel but changes no output is a lie — to the user, and to the AI specialist that claims to tune it. So we made a rule: there are no phantom knobs.

Every tunable parameter on every service must move the analysis. This isn't a promise, it's an enforced invariant: an automated sweep perturbs every dial across every service and field-device source and fails the build if any one leaves the output unchanged. Writing this paper's tooling caught real offenders — an eviction policy, a refresh interval, a Kubernetes oversubscription ratio, all of which rendered but computed nothing — and they were wired into the model until the sweep went green.

The invariant

Perturb any dial, and some output must move. An automated sweep holds the line, so the specialist personas that tune your nodes are reasoning about levers that genuinely bite.

05How accurate is it?

The honest answer starts by refusing a dishonest one. A sizing tool is not a load test, and pretending every output is accurate to a few percent would be the first lie. So we set the accuracy bar per metric — before collecting a single benchmark — and hold each output only to what is achievable.

MetricTarget accuracyWhy
storage±10% — near exactIt's arithmetic: records × bytes × replicas. No excuse to be wrong.
cost±20–30%List prices are public; the spread is instance choice, not model error.
capacity~±2× (order of magnitude)Real ceilings depend on hardware a sizing tool doesn't model.
latencydirectional + ballparkRegime-dependent and contention-driven; right shape, right order.

"Accurate" then means something checkable: each output lands inside its declared tolerance around a cited reference point. We keep a versioned, sourced benchmark corpus and run it against the live models on every change. It has two tiers:

  • Anchors — where the model's semantics match the source exactly: storage arithmetic, and vendor definitions like "1 DynamoDB RCU = one strongly-consistent 4 KB read per second." Held to a tight band; a hard test. These are the accuracy proof.
  • References — cited real-world targets from TPC-C/pgbench, YCSB, ANN-Benchmarks, Nexmark, Confluent throughput guides and cloud list pricing. Validated at order of magnitude; a value out of band is reported as a calibration gap, not hidden.

We publish our gaps

The corpus is honest about what isn't calibrated yet. It currently flags that our stream-processor capacity ceiling is roughly two orders of magnitude too low — it shares a constant tuned for heavy warehouse queries — and that gap sits in the test output with a note, not swept under the rug. A model that names its own errors is one you can trust with the ones it doesn't.

Each model card in the product carries the same distinction: a number is labelled anchored when it's tied to arithmetic or a vendor definition, and plausible when it's a defensible engineering estimate awaiting a benchmark. Knowing which is which is itself part of proving accuracy.

06From synthetic to observed

Everything above calibrates the models against the industry — TPC, YCSB, list prices. The next step is to calibrate them against your production environment, using the telemetry it already emits, and turn the Workbench into a digital twin you can run what-if scenarios against.

Modern platforms are already instrumented. OpenTelemetry emits, in one open format, exactly the two things our models need: the configuration and topology of a system, and its real behaviour. Resource attributes describe each service and its host, cloud, database and Kubernetes context. Traces reconstruct the call graph — which service calls which, at what latency and throughput. Metrics carry the truth of the running system: request rates, error rates, queue depth, utilisation, and p50/p99 latency histograms. That is a design, drawn by reality instead of by hand.

Observe

Ingest OTLP — metrics, traces and resource attributes — from the live platform. No new instrumentation; we read what OpenTelemetry already exports.

Reconstruct

Build the graph from what's observed: resource attributes become node configs, and the trace-derived service map becomes the edges. The architecture is discovered, not drawn.

Calibrate

Fit each model's free constants so its computed p50/p99, throughput and utilisation reproduce the observed metrics within tolerance. The generic model becomes a twin of this specific environment.

Validate

Hold out a slice of the telemetry and check the calibrated twin predicts it. Report the residual — the twin comes with a stated confidence, not a blind claim.

Ask "what if"

Now perturb it with grounding. Double the pods, add a read replica, switch consistency, absorb 3× the fleet — and the model predicts the delta from a baseline that already matches your last thirty days.

This is the payoff of insisting on transparent, tunable models from the start. A black-box simulator can't be calibrated to reality; a set of open formulas with real, load-bearing dials can. The same knobs the sweep proved genuine become the fitting parameters, and the same benchmark discipline becomes the validation gate. What-if scenarios stop being educated guesses and start being predictions with a confidence interval, anchored to the environment you actually run.

Telemetry isn't the only door into a running estate. The Workbench also reads the infrastructure directly — a Docker daemon, a Kubernetes cluster, an Azure resource group — and reverses the deployment map: each image or ARM resource becomes a node, and the tuned dials are recovered from its command, environment, SKU and resource limits. Read-only, and only what you point it at. This is the exact inverse of the same dial-to-deployment map that ships the design out to Compose, Kubernetes, Bicep, Terraform or Ansible.

That closes a loop back to §04. Discovery captures the full observed configuration — not just the dials we already model — and reports the difference: for each service type, the real settings we don't yet expose. Those become the candidate knobs to promote — made real, passed through the no-phantom sweep, and handed to the specialist that tunes them. Scan reality, see the gap, close it. The model doesn't merely calibrate to your environment; it grows to describe it.

Two ways in, one twin

Discovery recovers the services and their tuning; the OpenTelemetry import wires the call graph and the observed latencies. Together they rebuild a live twin — topology, configuration and behaviour — from a system that already exists.

Calibrate to the industry to be credible. Calibrate to your telemetry to be certain.

07What it is, and isn't

The Workbench is a modelling tool, not a load generator. It will not replace measuring your real system — it will tell you what to expect before you build it, and, once calibrated, what changing it will do.

Our commitments are the same four that run through this paper:

  • Show the work. Every number opens to a formula you can read and argue with.
  • Cite the source. Accuracy is claimed only against a named benchmark, within a declared tolerance.
  • No phantom knobs. Every dial moves the analysis, and a test proves it.
  • Publish the gaps. What isn't calibrated yet is labelled, not hidden.

A model that shows its work, cites its sources, and names its own errors is not merely more honest than a diagram. It is the only kind you can safely calibrate to reality — and reality is where the interesting questions live.
