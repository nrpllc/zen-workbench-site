Reading the process numbers

Zen puts six indicators on every process you model. They come from Six Sigma, which has a reputation for being a week of training. The idea underneath it takes about six minutes — and once you have it, the numbers stop being decoration and start telling you where to go.

Sections 1 to 5 are that six minutes. Sections 6 to 13 are for anyone who has already played with this and found the usual explanations a bit thin — what Zen actually counts, where the 1.5σ convention comes from, what it deliberately does not do, and the arithmetic that decides which improvement is worth making.

The full training set Product page
01

A process is a chain, and chains multiply

Every step in a process gets some of its work right the first time. That number is the step's first-pass yield. The whole chain is not the average of those numbers — it is the product of them. That single fact is most of Six Sigma.

What it means

Six respectable-looking steps produce a process that gets it right barely half the time. No single step looks broken, which is exactly why this goes unnoticed.

What to do

Stop judging steps individually. Judge the chain, then fix the weakest link — Zen names it for you under Sigma level.

02

Rolled yield, sigma level and DPMO are one number in three costumes

Zen shows rolled yield as a percentage, sigma level on the 0–6 scale, and DPMO — defects per million opportunities. They are conversions of the same quantity, offered because different audiences think in different units. Finance hears the percentage; a quality engineer hears the sigma; an operations lead hears the DPMO.

What it means

The scale is deliberately unforgiving at the top: each sigma is roughly an order of magnitude fewer defects. Moving 3σ → 4σ is a ten-fold reduction, not a 33% improvement.

What to do

Treat 3σ as the floor for anything a customer touches. Below it, the rework is loud enough that people have already built workarounds you are paying for.

03

Cycle time and flow efficiency: the wait is the problem

Cycle time is work plus wait. Flow efficiency is the share that was actually work. Five to fifteen per cent is normal — meaning most of what a customer calls “slow” is queue, not effort. Zen warns below 15%.

What it means

Making people work faster attacks the 9%. Removing a queue attacks the 91%. Almost every “we need more people” conversation is aimed at the wrong bar.

What to do

Find the longest wait before on any step and ask what it is waiting for — an approval, a batch, a person who is in meetings. That is your cycle time.

04

Handoffs: every crossing is a queue and a chance to drop

Zen counts how many handoffs cross a lane — that is, pass between roles — and warns at four. A crossing is where work waits for someone else's attention, and where context gets lost in the retelling.

What it means

A process where every handoff crosses a role boundary has no ownership. It is a relay in which nobody is running more than one leg.

What to do

Merge adjacent steps into one lane, or give one role end-to-end accountability. Zen shows the accountable role and the reporting line on every step.

05

The order to act in

The indicators are not a scorecard, they are a route. This is the order that pays.

Why this order

Yield first, because rework inflates everything downstream — including the wait you were about to go and fix. Then flow. Then structure.

The Zen part

Each of these is a dial on the model, not a workshop. Change it and every number moves at once, so you can see whether the idea was worth doing before you do it.

The longer version

06

What Zen counts as a defect — and what it does not

Zen's per-step number is first-pass yield: the share of work that clears the step without going round again. Rolled yield is those multiplied along the chain, and the “DPMO” on the strip is (1 − rolled yield) × 1,000,000.

If you have done this before, you will notice that is defective units per million, not classical DPMO — which divides by the number of opportunities for a defect in each unit. That is a deliberate choice: opportunity-counting is where Six Sigma numbers go to be gamed, since inflating the opportunity count flatters the result. Zen's version is comparable to itself over time, which is what you need to steer. It is not directly comparable to a DPMO someone quotes from a manufacturing line.

What it means

Your sigma here is a yield statement about whole units of work — a claim, a ticket, an onboarding — not about how many ways one unit could go wrong.

What to do

Never benchmark this against an external DPMO. Benchmark it against your own last quarter, which is the only comparison that survives scrutiny anyway.

07

Where the 1.5σ comes from

A yield of 93.3% is 1.5 standard deviations from the mean of a normal distribution. Zen — like everyone else — reports it as 3σ. The extra 1.5 is Motorola's long-term drift allowance: processes measured over weeks perform worse than the same processes measured over an afternoon, so the convention builds in a penalty rather than letting a short study flatter you.

It is a convention, not mathematics. It is also universal, which is why “six sigma” means 3.4 defects per million rather than the 0.002 per million the raw distribution implies.

08

What this is not

Zen computes attribute sigma — pass or fail, counted. It does not do control charts, Cp/Cpk capability indices, measurement system analysis, or designed experiments. There is no variance model underneath: a step is 85% good, not “85% good ± 6”.

If your process emits a continuous measurement against a specification limit — a diameter, a temperature, a latency SLO — that is variables-based capability, and it is a different toolkit. What Zen gives you is the flow-and-yield half, which is the half most business processes live in.

Why it still earns its place

Most office processes have never been measured at all. Going from “nobody knows” to “53%, and here is the worst step” is the expensive part. Control charts can come later, if ever.

Where to be careful

Do not present this sigma to someone who lives in Cp/Cpk without saying which one it is. They are different claims about different things.

09

Why fixing your worst step barely moves the number

This is the one that changes behaviour. Take the demo process: 59% rolled yield, 1.7σ, worst step at 85% first-pass yield. Fix that step — properly, all the way to 99% — and here is what the whole process does:

Worst step goes toRolled yieldProcess sigmaVerdict

A quarter of work, a heroic effort on the worst offender, and the process is still under 2σ. The other five steps were never the story you were told, but they were always the story.

What it means

Multiplication is unsentimental. With six steps you need 98.85% each to reach 3σ. There is no single hero fix.

What to do

Stop running improvement projects on one step. Either lift the whole chain, or shorten the chain — which is section 10.

10

Removing a step is the underrated lever

You cannot always make people better. You can often give the work fewer chances to fail.

What it means

Three steps removed buys you twenty points of yield without a single person improving. Approvals that approve nothing, forms nobody reads, a review that duplicates the one before it — those are yield.

What to do

Walk the swimlane and ask of each step: what would break if this did not exist? Zen will tell you what it does to the number before you commit.

11

Little's Law, and why the queue is a choice

Cycle time = work in progress ÷ throughput. That relationship is why a team that starts fewer things finishes them faster, and why “we're all very busy” and “nothing is coming out” live together comfortably.

Zen does not compute your WIP — it has no view of how many items you have in flight. What it does show is wait before on every step, and at 9% flow efficiency the wait is the cycle time. Little's Law is the reasoning that tells you what to do about it.

What it means

Adding people raises throughput a little and WIP a lot. The ratio often gets worse.

What to do

Cap what is in flight before hiring. It is free, and it moves the number Zen is already showing you.

12

What the rework is costing you

This is where the process half of Zen meets the money half. The demo organisation runs at $1.6M a month in people and process. Its flagship process has 41% rework.

Those two numbers do not multiply into a bill — a reworked item rarely costs exactly what the first pass cost, and not every hour in the organisation is spent on that process. But they bound the conversation. If even half of that rework lands on paid capacity, you are looking at a six-figure monthly sum being spent on doing things a second time, and it has never appeared as a line item because nobody bills “again”.

The hidden factory

Rework does not show up as a defect report. It shows up as headcount, as “that's just how long it takes”, and as the meeting where the same three people re-decide something.

What to do

Put the yield number and the cost number on the same slide. Quality arguments lose; capacity arguments win.

The honest caveat

Each sigma costs more than the last: 3σ→4σ is 11× fewer defects, 4σ→5σ is 27×, 5σ→6σ is 77×. Most business processes should stop at 4σ and spend the effort elsewhere.

The Zen part

All five moves are dials on the model. Try them before you try them on people.
