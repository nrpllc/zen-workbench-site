Reading the process numbers

Zen puts six indicators on every process you model. They come from Six Sigma, which has a reputation for being a week of training. The idea underneath it takes about six minutes — and once you have it, the numbers stop being decoration and start telling you where to go.

The full training set Product page
01

A process is a chain, and chains multiply

Every step in a process gets some of its work right the first time. That number is the step's first-pass yield. The whole chain is not the average of those numbers — it is the product of them. That single fact is most of Six Sigma.

What it means

Six respectable-looking steps produce a process that gets it right barely half the time. No single step looks broken, which is exactly why this goes unnoticed.

What to do

Stop judging steps individually. Judge the chain, then fix the weakest link — Zen names it for you under Sigma level.

02

Rolled yield, sigma level and DPMO are one number in three costumes

Zen shows rolled yield as a percentage, sigma level on the 0–6 scale, and DPMO — defects per million opportunities. They are conversions of the same quantity, offered because different audiences think in different units. Finance hears the percentage; a quality engineer hears the sigma; an operations lead hears the DPMO.

What it means

The scale is deliberately unforgiving at the top: each sigma is roughly an order of magnitude fewer defects. Moving 3σ → 4σ is a ten-fold reduction, not a 33% improvement.

What to do

Treat 3σ as the floor for anything a customer touches. Below it, the rework is loud enough that people have already built workarounds you are paying for.

03

Cycle time and flow efficiency: the wait is the problem

Cycle time is work plus wait. Flow efficiency is the share that was actually work. Five to fifteen per cent is normal — meaning most of what a customer calls “slow” is queue, not effort. Zen warns below 15%.

What it means

Making people work faster attacks the 9%. Removing a queue attacks the 91%. Almost every “we need more people” conversation is aimed at the wrong bar.

What to do

Find the longest wait before on any step and ask what it is waiting for — an approval, a batch, a person who is in meetings. That is your cycle time.

04

Handoffs: every crossing is a queue and a chance to drop

Zen counts how many handoffs cross a lane — that is, pass between roles — and warns at four. A crossing is where work waits for someone else's attention, and where context gets lost in the retelling.

What it means

A process where every handoff crosses a role boundary has no ownership. It is a relay in which nobody is running more than one leg.

What to do

Merge adjacent steps into one lane, or give one role end-to-end accountability. Zen shows the accountable role and the reporting line on every step.

05

The order to act in

The indicators are not a scorecard, they are a route. This is the order that pays.

Why this order

Yield first, because rework inflates everything downstream — including the wait you were about to go and fix. Then flow. Then structure.

The Zen part

Each of these is a dial on the model, not a workshop. Change it and every number moves at once, so you can see whether the idea was worth doing before you do it.
