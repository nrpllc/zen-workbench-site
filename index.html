AI-first, not AI-bolted-on

The prompt is the primary interface.

Most tools added a chat box next to the canvas. Here the AI writes directly into the model — and everything it produces is validated against the real catalog before it is allowed to land.

You write

"An IoT ingest pipeline for 900 plate-reader cameras, with an image store, a search tier and a relational database. Reserve the steady-state capacity."

→
You get, in the model
  • Components from the real catalog — no invented services
  • Wired and laid out by dependency, not dumped on a canvas
  • Sized and costed against live rates
  • Latency, throughput and storage computed per node
  • Notes on the trade-offs it made, and what it left out
Validated before it lands. Invented VM sizes are rejected outright — an unknown SKU would silently price as zero, which is worse than refusing. Unknown disk types are dropped with a warning, data disks are capped at what the size actually supports, ExpressRoute snaps to a purchasable port speed, and a rack layout that would overlap in U space is refused wholesale. On any hard failure nothing is written at all.

Cloud-native · Azure

Design for Azure, priced from the live rate card.

Subscription, region, VNet, subnet, instance — the real hierarchy, with ExpressRoute as a first-class link and Container Registry broken out on its own.

  • LIVEEvery line says where its price came from. Live from the public Retail Prices API where the feed publishes it, a dated snapshot where it doesn't — an estimate is never passed off as current.
  • DSKDisks bill the way Azure bills. Fixed P/E/S tiers charge the whole provisioned tier — a 100 GB premium disk bills as a 128 GB P10. Premium v2 and Ultra charge per GB plus IOPS above a free baseline.
  • RIReservations modelled properly. A commitment is paid whether the VM runs or not, so duty cycle discounts pay-as-you-go and nothing else.
  • AZZone-aware resilience. Instances with no availability zone are flagged — Azure may co-locate them, so they are not independent, whatever the replica count says.

Open source · Hybrid

The same model, wherever it runs.

Postgres, Kafka, Cassandra, Elasticsearch, Redis, MinIO, Neo4j — modelled with their real knobs, whether they run on your steel, on Azure VMs, or as a managed service.

  • OSSEngines, not logos. Replication factor, shards, partitions, heap, page cache, refresh interval — the settings that actually change the answer.
  • HYBHybrid is first-class. Domains for on-prem, edge, Azure, AWS and GCP in one design, with cross-boundary links costed for egress and checked for exposure.
  • ERExpressRoute back to the estate — sized to the traffic, with the Metered versus Unlimited egress trade-off modelled rather than guessed.
  • CMPCompare designs side by side — the same workload on your hardware, on Azure, or split across both.

On-premises · Design & discovery

Down to the rack rail.

Capture or design the facility — sites, halls, racks, machines — and everything else derives. Power, heat, weight, floor loading, circuit loading, cable lengths and spend.

  • 2NRedundancy that means something. Under N+1 or 2N each feed must carry the whole rack alone — a rack at 60% on both feeds is a failure, not a pass.
  • KWBudgets are ceilings you can overrun — utility feed, UPS, generator, cooling, floor area, floor loading — and the binding constraint is named outright.
  • CBLCabling derives itself — media chosen by speed and distance, lengths from floor geometry, rounded up to stocked lengths, exportable as a pull list.
  • DOCA drawing set falls out of it — executive overview, floor plans, elevations, power schedules and the pull list, ready to print.

Why the layers have to connect

Your architecture diagram doesn't know what a rack is.

Set replication factor to 3 and every tool says the data is safe. But three replicas sharing one rack also share a PDU pair and a top-of-rack switch — and three VMs with no availability zone may share a building. The config says replicated. The infrastructure says restore from backup.

What the configuration claims

replicationFactor: 3

Three copies, fast failover, recovery in seconds. It is the only thing a layer-blind tool can see.

What the placement delivers

0 surviving copies

All three landed in one fault domain. One failure takes the feed, the switch and every copy — recovery is now a restore, a different RPO and a far longer RTO.

Zen models the placement, not just the config. Instances are grouped into fault domains — racks, halls and sites on-prem, availability zones and regions in Azure — and the resilience profile is downgraded when the layout cannot deliver what the configuration promises.

The modelling engine

Latency is not linear in load.

This is the difference between a diagram and a model. Zen uses the operational-analysis framework real capacity planning is built on — utilisation law, bottleneck law and M/M/1 queueing — then checks itself against published benchmarks.

  • ρ50% utilisation costs 2× service time. 90% costs 10×. 95% costs 20×. That cliff is why designs that look fine at average load fall over at peak.
  • RAMMemory is a real knob. Cache against working set sets the hit ratio, which sets physical I/O, which sets disk utilisation, which sets latency.
  • p99The tail is derived — the exponential tail over the mean, ln(100) ≈ 4.6× — not an invented constant.
  • BTLRelieve the bottleneck and it moves. Enough RAM to cache the working set turns a disk-bound node CPU-bound, and the model says so.
